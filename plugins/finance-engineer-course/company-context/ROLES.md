# Meridian — Role Archetypes

These four archetypes represent the primary audience for this course. They are not specific individuals — they're patterns. If you work in ops, admin, marketing, or people at Meridian, you'll recognize yourself in at least one of them (and probably parts of several).

---

## Archetype 1: The Operations Coordinator

**Who They Are**

The Operations Coordinator is the person who keeps the machine running. They own the internal project tracker, manage the cadence of recurring meetings, document processes, and coordinate across functions when something needs to happen but no one person fully owns it. They're the first call when a vendor relationship needs managing, when a process needs to be written down, or when something is falling through the cracks. They tend to be highly organized, detail-oriented, and deeply familiar with how the business actually works — sometimes more so than leadership.

At Meridian, this person sits at the center of internal operations. They know the state of every open project, they're in more meetings than almost anyone, and they're often the one writing the follow-up email after a meeting where decisions were made but nothing was documented.

**Daily Tools**
Notion (project tracker, task lists, SOPs), Slack (cross-functional coordination), Google Calendar (meeting management), Google Docs (process documentation), Gmail (vendor and internal comms)

**Biggest Time Wasters**
- Writing and rewriting status updates that are essentially the same email every week
- Taking raw verbal processes from a colleague's explanation and turning them into clean SOPs from scratch
- Tracking down the status of action items that were buried in meeting notes or Slack threads
- Reformatting and cleaning up documentation that was created inconsistently

**What a Great Claude Use Case Looks Like**
Paste in a messy Slack thread from #ops and ask Claude to extract the action items, identify who owns what, and draft a clean follow-up summary. Or describe a process verbally and ask Claude to produce a first-draft SOP in the team's standard format. The goal is turning raw input — a thread, a conversation, a rough list — into a clean, shareable artifact in under 5 minutes.

## Archetype 2: The Executive Assistant / Chief of Staff Support

**Who They Are**

This person works directly in service of executive leadership. Their job is to make leadership more effective — by managing the calendar, drafting communications on behalf of the executive, preparing briefs before important meetings, and making sure action items from leadership conversations don't die in someone's inbox. They are the executive's second brain.

At Meridian, this role blurs with CoS work. The person in this archetype is not just a scheduler — they are a thought partner, a communicator, and an operator. They often know things before the executive does (because they've read the email, reviewed the calendar, and talked to the team) and their job is to synthesize that information and present it in the right form at the right time.

**Daily Tools**
Google Calendar (executive scheduling, meeting prep), Gmail (drafting on behalf of leadership, inbox management), Notion (meeting briefs, action item tracking), Slack (rapid coordination), Fireflies (meeting transcripts), Google Docs (exec communications)

**Biggest Time Wasters**
- Drafting emails and messages from scratch when most of the content is just a polished version of something they already know
- Switching contexts constantly — going from a board prep document to scheduling a call to writing an announcement in the same hour
- Preparing meeting briefs that require pulling information from 4 or 5 different sources (calendar, email, Notion, Slack) and stitching it together manually
- Writing the same types of communications (meeting follow-ups, recap emails, scheduling confirmations) over and over in slightly different forms

**What a Great Claude Use Case Looks Like**
Paste a messy set of raw meeting notes and ask Claude to extract decisions, action items, and owners, then draft a clean follow-up email to attendees. Or give Claude the context of an upcoming client meeting and ask it to produce a one-page brief covering: who's attending, what was discussed last time, what needs to be addressed, and what the desired outcome is. The goal is eliminating the "blank page" problem on high-stakes communications and briefs.

## Archetype 3: The Marketing Coordinator

**Who They Are**

The Marketing Coordinator owns the content and communications engine at Meridian. This includes the content calendar, social posts, email campaigns, internal announcements, and any external-facing written materials that don't come directly from a consultant or executive. They are the voice of the brand — and they are responsible for making sure that voice sounds consistent whether the message is going to a prospective client, a current client, or the internal team.

At Meridian, this role is relatively new. Marketing is a function that's still being built. That means the Marketing Coordinator has a lot of latitude and a lot of surface area to cover, often without a lot of established templates or precedent to follow. They're building the playbook while running plays at the same time.

**Daily Tools**
Google Docs (drafting content), Notion (content calendar, planning), Slack (cross-functional coordination on campaigns), Gmail (campaign coordination, outreach), Canva or similar (visual asset coordination), LinkedIn (publishing)

**Biggest Time Wasters**
- Staring at a blank document trying to write the first line of a post, email, or announcement — the blank-page problem is real and it slows everything down
- Rewriting content that was drafted in the wrong tone — too casual, too stiff, too technical, not technical enough
- Adapting the same core message for multiple formats (a Slack announcement, a LinkedIn post, an email newsletter) without a starting point for each
- Getting feedback that something "doesn't sound like us" without clear guidance on what that means

**What a Great Claude Use Case Looks Like**
Provide a topic, a few bullet points of what needs to be said, and the audience — then ask Claude to draft a LinkedIn post, a short email, and a Slack announcement all from the same brief. Or paste a draft that feels off-tone and ask Claude to rewrite it in a more specific direction. The goal is never to let Claude replace the marketing coordinator's judgment, but to eliminate the friction between "knowing what to say" and "having something to edit."

## Archetype 4: The People and Culture Coordinator

**Who They Are**

The People and Culture Coordinator is responsible for the human side of the business: onboarding new hires, tracking team milestones (birthdays, work anniversaries, promotions), communicating policy changes, and handling the internal communications that require both precision and warmth. This is a role where the stakes of getting the tone wrong are high — a cold onboarding message or a poorly worded policy update can create confusion, anxiety, or a sense that leadership doesn't care.

At Meridian, this person often operates in the space between HR and communications. They're not just policy enforcers — they're culture builders. They think about how announcements will land, how new hires feel on their first week, and how to communicate hard things in a way that's honest without being harsh.

**Daily Tools**
Notion (team directory, onboarding trackers, people ops documentation), Gmail (official HR communications, offer letters, policy notices), Slack (culture touchpoints, team announcements, check-ins), Google Docs (onboarding materials, policy documents), Google Calendar (scheduling onboarding sessions, 1:1s)

**Biggest Time Wasters**
- Writing the same types of messages over and over in slightly different forms — onboarding welcome emails, check-in messages, policy reminder communications — each time from a blank slate
- Calibrating tone for sensitive situations: how formal is too formal? How warm is too casual for a professional communication?
- Translating a policy or decision from "what it is" to "how to announce it in a way that people will receive well"
- Making sure consistent information (onboarding steps, benefits FAQs, policy details) gets included every time without building a full template system

**What a Great Claude Use Case Looks Like**
Describe a situation ("we're announcing a new PTO policy, some team members may feel it's a reduction, we want to be transparent about why we made the change") and ask Claude to draft a team announcement that's honest, clear, and warm. Or provide the key facts about a new hire and ask Claude to draft a personalized welcome email and first-week Slack introduction. The goal is having a strong first draft for every human-communication moment so that the People Coordinator can focus on the thinking, not the typing.
